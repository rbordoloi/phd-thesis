2.1 Iterative Imputation

One of the earliest major advancements in multivariate imputation was by [1], called MICE. MICE is a general approach that starts off from a naive imputation, and then iteratively fits a univariate regression model on each feature to impute the missing feature values. It is computationally efficient, highly adaptable as it can be used with any regression model. It can also specify different regression models for each features, and this can be used for all types of tabular data, be it numeric, categorical, or mixed. Because of this flexibility and computational efficiency, MICE remains one of the most widely adopted imputation methods in the literature. However, MICE starts being computationally inefficient when the datasets have large numbers of features, because it requires multiple independent models to be fit for each feature iteratively.

Another highly successful approach to imputation has been ensemble methods, such as HyperImpute[2] and RF-GAP[3]. HyperImpute attempts to make the iterative imputation process more flexible, by allowing a model search using AutoML[4] methods over multiple regression models for each feature. On the other hand, RF-GAP defines a novel proximity measure on an ensemble of binary recursive trees, and computes a proximity-based sum to impute missing values.

2.2 Generative Imputation

Another line of research views imputation as a generative process. The idea is to estimate the conditional distribution of the missing values given the observed ones, and sample from this distribution to impute missing values. Generative models usually sample from a simple, known distribution, like a multivariate normal distribution, and transform these samples to match the required conditional distribution. The mechanism of transformation and the assumed form of the conditional distribution separates these models.

One of the simplest methods to do this is to adapt a Deep Latent Variable Model (DLVM) to generate imputations for missing values. DLVMs draw samples (known as the latent variable) from a simple known prior distribution, usually a Gaussian. They then attempt to approximate the posterior distribution of the observed values over this latent variable by fitting a parameterized family of distributions conditioned on it. This family is usually parameterized as a simple family (like a Gaussian), over a parameterized transformation of the latent variable, usually via a neural network. Sampling from this posterior is often either intractable or has an impractical convergence rate, so amortized variational inference is used to estimate another parameterized proposal distribution that is as close as possible to the posterior. These approaches have been explored in works like [5] and [6], for different assumptions on the missingness mechanism.

Another early approach was to modify a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) to generate imputations for missing values. The missing values are initially imputed by draws from a simple distribution like a Gaussian, and then the generator network is used to transform these samples to match the required conditional distribution. An adversarial discriminator network is then trained to estimate the corresponding missingness mask, and then the generator and discriminator are adversarially trained iteratively. This results in imputed that values that the discriminator is unable to discern as imputed.

Another class of generative models that have been found to be very successful in image tasks are diffusion models. Diffusion models generate data by iteratively corrupting data samples with noise from a simple known distribution, usually Gaussian, and learning a noise function to predict the amount of noise in a given corrupted sample. The diffusion model then takes as input a sample that is purely noise, and runs this corruption process in reverse using the learned noise function, to generate a sample from the data distribution. Due their impressive generative modeling capacity, many imputation approaches based on them have been developed in recent years. One of the first approaches in this direction was Conditional Score-Based Diffusion (CSDI) models[7], which attempt to impute values at missing time points in multivariate time-series data by conditioning the diffusion model on the observed time points and then denoising from white noise using the conditional diffusion model. Another similar approach (Conditional Schrödinger Bridge for Imputation)[8] constructs and solves a Schrödinger Bridge Problem (SBP) with conditional constraints to impute missing values in time-series data, where the conditional constraints are derived from the observed values. The SBP constructs a simple marginal prior on the distribution of time-series trajectories, usually Brownian motion or an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, and tries to find the minimum optimal transport path between the marginal distribution of the observed samples and this marginal prior. Similar to diffusion models, one then samples form this simple marginal prior and transforms the samples in reverse through this path to resemble the observed sample distribution and impute the missing values. However, both these approaches are only available for imputation in multivariate time-series data.

An interesting approach for tabular data (CSDI_T)[9] consists of removing the temporal transformer layers from CSDI[7] and constructing embeddings for categorical variables, after which the same diffusion procedure as CSDI is applied. Another diffusion-based approach(Forest Diffusion)[10] constructs multiple copies of the dataset with different noise samples added, on which gradient-boosted trees are trained to perform the reverse diffusion process to reconstruct the missing data values. The NewImp[11] model, on the other hand, attempts to learn the missing value distribution by learning the score function of the joint distribution using a Denoising Score Matching approach and then by stepwise reconstructing the missing values using an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) simulation of the joint distribution based on these learned scores. Another similar approach, MissDiff[12], attempts to learn a score-based model by modifying the loss in a conventional Variance Preserving Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) for diffusion models to only consider the observed values.

2.3 Other Loss-based Methods for Imputation

Loss-based methods for imputation often involve optimizing for a cleverly constructed loss function that attempts to approximate the imputation error. A major challenge in these approaches is that very often in imputation tasks, the ground truth values are unavailable, and thus cannot be used in the loss function. One simple solution to this is to synthetically mask an additional section of the observed values, and compute the loss using those synthetically masked values as the ground truth.

For instance, a hierarchical variational autoencoder-based approach (HH-VAEM)[13] generates the missing data by training multiple separate variational autoencoders to encode the latent space of each feature and the dependencies among them. This training is helpful in the presence of diverse feature types. ReMasker[14] presents a transformer-based encoder-decoder network that attempts to reconstruct the synthetically masked values, using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. Similarly, MIRACLE[15] uses a simple feed-forward neural network to improve naively imputed values. Its loss function is a sum of three terms: a reconstruction term which attempts to reconstruct both the missing values and the missingness mask, and two causal terms which ensure that the inter-variable dependencies form a causal structure as represented by a directed acyclic graph. Another approach based on a modification of OTImputer[16] attempts to minimize the [17] between latent representations of the original and imputed data set generated via a neural network, where the latent representation generating neural network is fit to maximize the Mutual Information to prevent model collapse.

GRAPE is a somewhat more novel approach[18], which consists of representing the data set as a labeled bipartite graph, with the two node types representing the samples and the features, and each edge label representing the value of the particular feature for that sample. One then uses a GraphSAGE[19] inspired Graph Neural Network to predict the missing edge labels, which are the imputed values.

2.4 Impact of Imputation on Downstream Task Performance

[20] shows that simply improving imputation often offers limited benefits for downstream predictive performance. Their study of the impact of four different imputation methods on 3 different classifiers across 19 datasets under the Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) mechanism showed that good imputations matter very little for more expressive predictors. In particular, they found that gains in prediction \(R^2\) is at most \(10\%\) of the gains in imputation \(R^2\). Moreover, they also found that adding the missingness indicator as a feature makes the quality of imputation matter less. Additionally, a similar effect is also observed when the response is non-linearly dependent on the input. However, imputation becomes more important when the missingness mechanism is more complicated, like the self-masking model of Missing Not at Random (MNAR), as described in Remark 1.2.

Because of this, some lines of research focus on developing methods that perform classification or regression without explicitly imputing the missing values. An example of this is NeuMiss networks[21], which works by unrolling an iterative approximation of the covariance matrix using a cleverly designed neural network. This network can then be composed with any loss-based downstream objective, such that the overall model learns without imputation.

Methods in short-time series analysis usually attempt to learn complete features from fragmented or missing data, that can then be used by classical machine learning algorithms for downstream tasks. A prominent example is Functional Linear Discriminant Analysis (FLDA)[22], which projects the observed univariate time series trajectories onto a functional basis, and performs linear discriminant analysis on these basis coefficients to compute representation vectors. This method is discretization-invariant with respect to the time variable and only learns from the observed values. Some extensions of this method exist for multivariate time-series as well [23], [24], but they do not consider missingness in the feature dimension, only in the temporal dimension like [22].
